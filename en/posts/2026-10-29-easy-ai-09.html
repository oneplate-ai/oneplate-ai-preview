Is It Safe to Put
Personal Information into AI?

When you ask AI to edit or organize something, you may want to include names, contact details, or the full document.
This lesson shows what to remove and which privacy and retention conditions to check first.

Personal information can identify a person. FTC guidance

Personal information is information that identifies someone directly, or that can identify them when combined with other details.
Examples can include a phone number, address, email, account number, face photo, health record, school, or workplace information.

A single detail may look harmless, but several ordinary details together can reveal who someone is.
Do not assume that removing a name is enough; ask whether the remaining material could still point to a specific person.

Start by keeping only what the task needs.

If you want AI to improve a sentence, it usually does not need a real name, phone number, or address.
Replace “Alex Kim” with “Customer A,” and remove account numbers, government ID numbers, passwords, and medical details unless the task truly requires them and the service is approved for that use.

The same applies to workplace or school documents.
Having permission to read a document does not automatically give you permission to upload it to an AI service, so check your organization’s rules and the document’s allowed use first.

Check the service’s data controls and retention terms separately. OpenAI guidance

Services can differ in how they save chats, use content to improve models, handle memory, review content, and process deletion requests.
Do not assume that every AI service treats personal information in the same way; read the official settings and privacy notice for the service you are using.

For example, personal ChatGPT accounts can manage whether conversations are used to improve models under Settings → Data Controls.
Turning that setting off does not automatically delete saved chats, and temporary chats can still be retained for safety, so “temporary” does not mean that no copy can exist.

A temporary chat or a changed setting is not the same as deletion.

Google’s official Gemini privacy guidance also distinguishes activity storage from processing needed to provide the service and protect users.
Even when activity saving is off, Google says it may still process chats to respond and to support safety, so one setting does not remove every privacy consideration.

Before entering sensitive information, reduce it first.
If you already entered it, check how the service handles chats, files, memories, and activity, and delete what you no longer need.
For a work or school account, administrator retention rules may take priority over personal settings.

Use these four checks when personal information is involved.

First, is the information necessary?
Keep only the part needed for the AI task and remove the rest.

Second, whose information is it?
Check permission and consent before uploading another person’s contact details, photo, or document.

Third, where is it stored and for how long?
Read the service’s official terms about storage, deletion, model improvement, and human review.

Fourth, did you clean up the copies afterward?
The original file, a copied file, and the AI result may all contain personal information, so remove unnecessary versions and chats.

The safest personal information is information you never send.

One-line takeaway.

Share only the minimum information, use placeholders when possible, and check each service’s storage, deletion, and model-improvement rules before sending sensitive data.
