Who Owns the Copyright
in an AI-Generated Result?

When AI creates text, images, or music, it can feel as if the result is automatically yours to use.
But using AI does not automatically create copyright, and it does not remove other people’s rights.
This lesson separates human creative contribution from the rights attached to the material you use.

Copyright protects creative works. U.S. Copyright Office guidance

Copyright is a set of rights that can protect creative works such as writing, images, and music.
It can give a creator control over ways others copy, distribute, or publicly use a work, but the scope and exceptions depend on the country and the type of work.

The fact that AI produced a result does not automatically give a person copyright in the whole result.
The U.S. Copyright Office explains that copyright analysis should distinguish the parts where a person contributed creative expression from the parts generated autonomously by an AI system.
This lesson uses the U.S. framework as an introduction, not as legal advice or a rule for every country.

A short prompt and a person’s own creation may be treated differently.

If you enter a short prompt such as “draw a seaside town” and receive an image, you supplied an idea and instructions, but you may not have directly chosen the final expressive details.
Whether the whole result qualifies as a human-authored work is not decided automatically.

If you create a draft, select parts of an AI result, change the composition, rewrite sentences, and combine elements through creative choices, the human contribution may be clearer.
But simply saying “I edited it” is not enough by itself; the relevant question is what creative decisions the person actually made.

Check the rights in the material you provide to AI.

Putting your own photo, writing, or music into an AI tool is different from uploading someone else’s material without permission.
If you use another person’s image, character, voice, or document, check permission, licensing terms, and attribution requirements first.

Even when a service says that its output may be used commercially, that does not automatically resolve third-party copyright, trademark, likeness, or voice rights in the result.
Before publishing, check whether the result is unusually similar to a known work or person, keep source records, and have a person review it.

Record these four things before you publish.

First, what did you create yourself?
Keep the draft, photo, composition, wording, and editing decisions that show your creative contribution.

Second, what did you provide to the AI?
Record the sources and usage terms for the text, images, or audio you supplied, and verify permission or licensing for other people’s material.

Third, which service and settings did you use?
Save the service name, date, important settings, generation steps, and later edits.

Fourth, what did a person review before publication?
Check similarity, rights concerns, factual errors, personal information, and commercial-use conditions before sharing the result.

A label saying “AI-generated” does not settle every rights question.
Start by recording the human creative contribution and the sources of the material you used.

One-line takeaway.

Do not assume an AI result is automatically yours; check the human creative contribution, source rights, attribution, and publication conditions, then keep a record.
